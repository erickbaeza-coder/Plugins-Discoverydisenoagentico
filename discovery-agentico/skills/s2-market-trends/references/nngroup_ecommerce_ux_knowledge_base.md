Executive Summary

Yes, this is a summary of the summary. Considering that the full Ecommerce
User Experience report series clocks in at more than 2000 pages with more
than 1700 screenshots, the more summaries the merrier.
#### Overall Good Usability
In our first ecommerce studies, during the dot-com bubble, users failed
almost half the time when they tried to shop on ecommerce sites. No wonder
the bubble burst with sites that bad. Now, users fail much less often. In our
most recent study, the majority of tasks were successful. Of course, not
every activity was seamlessly completed as we observed plenty of usability
issues along the way. Sites are still leaving money on the table.
Today, our main reason to recommend usability improvements for
ecommerce sites is the competitive pressure from other sites that keep
getting better. Plus, in recent years, ecommerce powerhouses like Amazon
are pushing boundaries and drastically influence user’s expectations for
service. Yes, there’s also an argument for improving design purely to reduce
user failures, but this reason is not as critical as it was in the past. Today’s
consumers are not satisfied with sites that simply make it possible to shop.
The experience must also be pleasant, fast, and convenient, so we should
look beyond success rates, as the ability to complete tasks remains the first
priority in the line of requirements.
Some of the most notable improvements we observed in our research for the
fourth edition of this report are in areas that will help ecommerce retailers
compete in the area of customer experience. We saw improvements in the
quality of product information offered to shoppers. More sites are meeting
user’s information needs with helpful and detailed product descriptions.
They’ve supplemented rich descriptions with other helpful informational
features like robust review functionality, product question and answer
forums, and more advanced image capabilities to help users inspect the
finest details of the items they’re considering. In addition to improvements in
the information provided to customers were improvements outside of the
interface itself with the capabilities and services stores provided to
customers. Sites are offering more choices and flexibility to customers in
terms of payment options, shipping and delivery options, options for setting
up recurring orders, and even to select how they prefer to receive customer
support. This level of flexibility allows customers to interact on their terms
and design a shopping experience that suits their needs. We also saw

Mobile site optimization has been widely adopted, and the quality of these

Site search could still be better, even though it has steadily improved over

#### Many Old Findings Stand
The first and second editions of this report series were based on lab-based
usability testing, diary studies, and eyetracking studies. The research for
these editions was conducted in 3 countries: the US, Denmark, and China
and more than 200 sites were studied. The third edition of this report was
based on expert reviews of new site designs based on existing guidelines.
With this, the fourth edition, many of the guidelines from the previous
editions still stand today. With the creation of this most recent edition, we
have decided to retire a collection of guidelines that the industry has
“outgrown”. These guidelines were no longer necessary because we did not
observe the issues that led to them in our most recent research or because
the web has changed enough that we deemed them unhelpful (more about
what has changed on page 13).
The fact that a large percentage of the old guidelines were still confirmed is
an indication of the longevity of usability findings. Our design
recommendations are based on the characteristics of the human mind which
change much more slowly than the technology that fascinates so many
people in the field.

## Vol.02: Homepage & Category Pages
**Tags:** homepage, category, navigation, product-listing, filters, sorting

### Hallazgos clave

Further information about each of the following topics and additional topics
can be found in the Homepage and Category Pages report in the Ecommerce
Report Series.
A Welcoming Homepage
The homepage is a crucial page on the site, as it acts as a user’s introduction
to the company, its products, and the site itself. The page needs to look
trustworthy, make clear what type of products are sold on the site, and invite
users to make a purchase.
A Clear Structure
A clear navigational structure is important for the obvious reason of letting
users locate products of interest. However, it is also essential for shoppers
who arrive at your site via a link to an interior page, such as through a
search engine, blog, email from a friend, social media, or some other source.
Remember that many shoppers will enter the site without seeing the
homepage. For these users, the site structure and navigational options
become the way they are introduced to the site and all it has to offer.
When users don’t know what navigational categories mean, or the differences
between them, they are unable to navigate to the right products on the site.
Users can’t buy what they can’t find.

78
Key Findings By Report

A user wasn’t sure where to go to shop for flowers to send to a friend in the
hospital on ProFlowers.com. The categories Sympathy and Get Well seemed too
similar, so he wasn’t sure which was best for his needs.
Products and product categories should be listed under each relevant
category or navigational option, so shoppers can find items in multiple
related categories. When users don’t find what they want in one category,
they don’t necessarily look to another related category. They assume if no
item is shown, no item exists.
A polyhierarchy can support these multiple ways users may choose to browse
and ensure that they find the right products no matter the path they take to
locate them.

79

The Asos site categorized Bags & Purses as its own subcategory, rather than as
a type of accessory, as one user expected. “So, I clicked on Women, I’m going
to go right to Accessories. And go right to, hmmm. I’m looking for something
that says bags or purses.” The site did not also list bags in the Accessories
section, which made them difficult for her to find.
Promoting Subcategories While Exposing Products
Category pages show users an overview of a specific section of the site, while
product listing pages show users the collection of items for sale within that
category or sub-category. In our most recent study, fewer sites provided
traditional category pages as interim pages before viewing individual product
listings than in the past.
In an effort to expose individual products sooner, many sites instead
immediately show product listing pages, with the ability for users to use
filters or facets as a means of narrowing their selection. The most successful
designs merged category pages and product listing pages, showing users an
overview of available subcategories above the product listing, separate from
other filters.

80
Key Findings By Report

The Chewy site presented links to product types within the Cat Treats category
above product listings. This allowed users to easily see available subcategories
to narrow their options or begin browsing products.
Providing Product Information
Product listing pages should offer enough information that shoppers can get
a sense of the product before clicking through to a full product page. Such
pages are often users’ first interaction with product information on a site. If
shoppers don’t see what they want on a product listing page, they won’t look
to product detail pages for more information. Clear images, cropped and
scaled so as to give a sense of the product, are essential as well as price
information and product name. When users recognized a product on the
product listing page, they sometimes added the item to the cart without even
viewing the full product detail page.
Supplying additional product photos directly on product listing pages is
another way to give more information to users to help them decide which
items to click on. Several sites allowed this by providing arrows or other
carousel controls for users to scroll through additional images, or by showing

81

a single alternate image on rollover. When implemented well, users
appreciated this ability to access more images.

A user on Airbnb.com appreciated being able to scroll through additional images
of homes on listing pages when looking for a place to stay.
Many sites also offer a quick view tool on product listing pages, which allows
users to view limited product information without navigating to the full detail
page. These quick view features worked best when users could clearly control
whether to click to open the quick view, or when to navigate to the product
detail page.
Product Comparisons
Users had their own ways of comparing products. Some took notes on paper.
Others opened multiple windows or tabs. And some used the comparison
tools provided by sites.
The most important thing sites can do to allow product comparison is to
provide comparable product information and equivalent levels of detail about
similar items on the site. If offering a full comparison tool, users must be
able to quickly identify the tool, easily select items to compare, make
comparisons of helpful information across products, and quickly remove
items, add items to the cart, or view product detail pages. Comparison tables
should highlight the differences between the selected products. Unclear or
incomplete comparison tables leave users wondering if features exist in a
product or not.

82
Key Findings By Report

Sorting Through Options
Getting users to the products that best fit their needs includes a combination
of navigation, search, and filtering. Faceted search, or narrowing options
according to attributes, is discussed on page 95 of this report and in the
Search report. Sorting is also crucial to move users to the right set of
products. Users in the study expected to be able to sort products by price, at
a minimum and by popularity, brand, customer rating, or length of time on
the site when appropriate.
Sorting by price was particularly popular with users. We saw this feature
used frequently when shoppers were trying to find an item on the site that
would add just enough to their purchase total to qualify them for a discount
or free shipping. Looking for a low-cost item to add was simpler when sites
allowed sorting by price.

## Vol.03: Product Pages
**Tags:** product-page, product-details, images, reviews, comparison, add-to-cart

### Hallazgos clave

Further information about each of the following topics and additional topics
can be found in the Product Pages report in the Ecommerce Report Series.
Full Product Details
The most important element of a product page is, of course, providing
product details. The product page is the only way a user can gather
information about the product he is considering. Descriptions need to be
complete and comprehensive, anticipating and answering users’
questions. A user browsed Reverb.com looking for a specific guitar pedal, but
he was disappointed to find a product description that used dramatic, flowery
language to describe the sound effects. He was so disappointed, that he left
the site and looked for reviews of the pedal on YouTube.
Product information also needed to be easy to find on the page. Many sites
used long scrolling pages to present product information, and often full
product details were located far down below the fold. This made it difficult for
customers to find information they wanted. Though we observed that users
are willing to scroll, some users struggled to find product details within long
scrolling pages, simply because there was so much information on the page.
Users can easily become overwhelmed with too much product information at
once. Layer product information so those who want minimal details or
distinguishing features can find them easily, but so those wanting specifics
have access to them as well. For example, some sites showed a list of key
product details at the top of the page, with more details available below. This
helped users who just wanted a quick understanding of an item, before
deciding if they wanted to look for more detail.

83

Product images are incredibly important. Users can’t see, touch or hold the
item they are purchasing online. Pictures have to show users as much as
possible about a product. Images from different angles or showing items in
context help users understand more about a product. Users also expected to
be able to zoom in on parts of an image for even more detail. Unfortunately,
even in this most recent study, some sites did not offer sufficient tools for
zooming in on images.
In addition to static images, many sites included product videos or short
animations. These were helpful to illustrate how a product worked, as long
as the motion began on user-initiated action. Some users happily watched
product videos (users in our study watched videos for a range of items
including a smart thermostat, a water bottle, a guitar pedal, and hair
extensions). As helpful as videos can be, sites shouldn’t rely on them,
because many users avoided videos or animations, because they didn’t trust
that the quality would be worthwhile, or they didn’t want to invest the time.
Sites should use videos or animations as a complement to static images and
descriptive text.
Smart Suggestions for Related Products
In this latest round of research, one of the more notable changes since
previous rounds was the increase of suggested products on product pages.
Many sites included product suggestions based on the product the user was
viewing. Common categories varied beyond a simple You might also like, to
include more data-driven categories such as: Customers who bought this
item also bought, Customers who viewed this also viewed, Frequently bought
with (for accessories or component parts), and Recently viewed. Shoppers
appreciated seeing these items on a product page, as long as they were
highly relevant to the item they were looking at.
Because this pattern of suggested products has become widely adopted,
some users complained if they weren’t available. One user on Sholastic.com
liked a book that was out of stock. She said,
“It would be cool if you could go and find something similar to
this, since it’s out of stock, without me necessarily having to go
back and look. But there’s nothing that looks like it comes up
that says, ‘Similar items to what you just searched for or looked
at’. So, I’m going to go back.”
Usable Product Reviews
Product reviews were incredibly helpful to users when making purchase
decisions. Providing customer reviews gave shoppers additional insight into
products, often answering questions shoppers had. Product reviews give

information about the use of a product, not just its features. As one user said
about the reviews on Macy’s site, “It had reviews and I needed that. There
was no opportunity to experience the product, other than other people’s
experience.”
Reviews have the potential to provide more detail than any product
description, increase trust of the site and the products for sale, and help cut
down on returns by setting more accurate expectations.
Some users looked only at summary review information, seeing what the
overall rating for a product was. Others had specific questions left
unanswered by the product description that they looked to the reviews to
answer. Others checked the reviews to see if the product description was
accurate according to customers.
Reviews had to be presented well to be helpful. Users wanted to be able to
get a quick sense of the number of reviews, and how many reviews were
favorable or unfavorable. They wanted quick access to the best and the worst
reviews. They also sometimes found it useful to know a little about the
person posting the review, such as age, gender or other pertinent details.
Beyond highlighting information about the person who posted the
review, customers appreciated the ability to have control over how they
could view the reviews. Tools to sort or filter by review rating or reviewer
characteristics were helpful.
Information in reviews seemed reliable to users, even though the information
was from strangers. Users did evaluate the quality of reviews, dismissing
those they felt were too positive or too negative, such as one negative review
among six positive reviews.
However, if an outlying review, which was overly positive or negative or
which focused on one product detail, matched the user’s questions or
concerns about that product, it could be highly influential.
Users were quick to doubt the credibility of reviews if they were hand-
picked by the site. They were also less trusting of reviews on a
manufacturer’s own site versus a site that sold products by many different
manufacturers.
Images or videos submitted as a part of a customer review were also
appreciated by our users. People wanted to know if an item matched its
description, so authentic customer images were persuasive.

85

Customer-submitted images added credibility to user reviews on Amazon’s site.
Product Availability and Arrival
Users wanted to know when they could expect to have the product.
Sometimes this was out of curiosity, but other times it impacted their
decision to purchase one item over another item. When sites provided
information about available inventory, or when an item would ship, or better
yet, when the item would arrive, users appreciated it.
On some sites, users could check in-store availability and in-store pickup for
nearby stores. These options were helpful for those users who knew a store
was convenient to get to, or for those that didn’t want to wait for an item to
be shipped.
Adding Items to the Cart
Users ran into a surprising amount of trouble adding items to shopping carts.
Many sites provided inadequate feedback when an item was added to the
cart, showing a momentary animation, adding a single line of text to a
product page, or changing a minor design element on the page.
When sites didn’t provide adequate feedback, some users thought they had
added an item to the cart when they had not, and others added items

86
Key Findings By Report

multiple times. While subtle page changes may be designed to keep the
shopper in the shopping environment, they actually often resulted in the user
leaving the product page to investigate the shopping cart and try to
determine what, if anything, had been added to the cart.
Shoppers didn’t necessarily need to see the entire cart on each page of the
site. But including an indication of the number of items in the cart or a
subtotal for the items in the cart was helpful to users. This prevented them
from having to check the cart, particularly if they were shopping within a
budget.

## Vol.04: Cart, Checkout & Registration
**Tags:** cart, checkout, registration, payment, shipping, forms, guest-checkout

### Hallazgos clave

Further information about each of the following topics and additional topics
can be found in the Shopping Carts, Checkout and Registration report in the
Ecommerce Report Series.
Minicart as Feedback, Not Cart Replacement
Providing a page in the checkout flow that is dedicated to displaying the full
cart helps users make their purchase decisions. They can use the cart to
review their selections, check details, compare items, and even finesse totals
to meet budgets or qualify for discounts.
Some sites only allow users to view the shopping cart in a minicart view that
appears as an overlay or as part of the checkout process, but not as a
dedicated page. For shoppers who are trying to consider a purchase or make
a decision, this can make decision making more difficult, as the cart is
relegated to a small area of the page.
Minicarts give users quick, but limited, access to information about what is in
the cart. They are typically small and only display a few items at a time.
They force the shopper to do more work to see items in the cart, and make
comparison between items more complicated, as users need to scroll the
small area of the page.
Minicarts are a useful way of showing feedback that an item has been
successfully added to the cart but are not a replacement for a full shopping
cart page or panel that allows users to manage items before purchase. Users
frequently wanted to change quantity, edit product options such as size or
color, and double-check items before checking out. A minicart is a poor
design for doing so.
Feedback that an item has been successfully added to the cart is critical, and
a minicart is a great way of affirmatively showing that an item has been
added. However, we do not recommend using a disappearing minicart that
quickly fades from view. In our usability testing, when users encountered

87

overlay style minicarts that would disappear automatically, they would often
disappear so quickly that the user didn’t have the opportunity to review the
message, causing quite a bit of frustration. One user on Burberry.com added
a coat to her cart, noticed the overlay showing that the product had been
added to her cart, and began moving her mouse over to the overlay to
double-check that the item was the size she had intended to add. However,
before she could read the overlay, it disappeared. “Where did my bag go? I
guess it’s really sensitive to the movement of my mouse.”

The overlay on Burberry.com showing when an item has been added to the
shopping bag disappears quickly. Users wishing to review the feedback to make
sure they had added the right item to their bag were frustrated by the
disappearing overlay.
Shopping Cart as Staging Area
Many of our users saw the shopping cart as an area to hold items they were
considering purchasing. An item in the cart did not necessarily mean the user
wanted to purchase the item; it meant users were considering an item or
wanted to remember it.
Many sites offer wishlists or favorite lists for this purpose, intending users to
create a list of items they are interested in. However, our users frequently
did not want to go through the trouble of setting up a list that required
registration. They simply wanted a quick and easy way to store items they
were interested in for quick access. While the shopping cart was the most
popular way to store items for later consideration, many sites in our study
did design a wishlist or favorites list well. Further details on designing a

88
Key Findings By Report

wishlist feature that users want to use in the Wishlists, Gift Cards, and Gift
Giving report in the Ecommerce Report Series.
Users wanted to be able to see clear product images, product names, product
details like size, color, and price in the shopping cart. This helped them
remember each product as well as compare between products. They also
expected products in the cart to have links back to full product pages, so
they could check or review product details when deciding what to ultimately
purchase.
Because shoppers were often adding items to the cart, only to remove them
later, it was essential that the site made it easy to remove items from the
cart, or edit various options such as size, color, or quantity.
A Clear Path Through Checkout
The checkout process should be focused on getting users through the
purchase. Excessive cross-promotions, extended warranty offers, overly
gimmicky encouragement messages, and marketing copy aimed at getting
users to register on the site all draw attention away from the user’s main
task at hand: giving you money.
The process of finding and purchasing items online is cognitively difficult
work and requires users to expend a lot of mental resources and keep a lot
of information in their working memory in order to complete a purchase.
Anything you can do to help reduce this mental workload on your users will
improve the chances that they will finalize their purchase and give you
money.
The most important thing you can do for your checkout process is to
streamline it, removing unnecessary steps, form fields, and stimulation
during the process. When users have begun the checkout process, this is the
point where it is the site’s sale to lose.
Single-page or Multi-page checkout
Some sites tried to put the entire checkout process on one page, either in a
long and complex form or using dynamic designs to build the entire purchase
flow on one page. This often resulted in distracting or confusing checkout
experiences, with form fields appearing, changing or disappearing when
users chose various options.
While the single-page checkout form has become popular with many sites
(ostensibly to simplify the process as much as possible), sites that
designed their checkout process on multiple pages worked equally
well. So long as users understood where they were in the process, what the
next step would be, and when their credit card would be charged, they were
successful proceeding through checkout.

89

Create shortcuts
Users wanted to see full product details, as mentioned above, as well as price
information early in the checkout process, and again at the end to double-
check before charging their credit card. They appreciated an indication of
shipping, or other additional fees early in the process.
One useful element for many users was the ability to indicate the user’s
billing and shipping address was the same; for most sites, this is a good
default state. Our users complained often and loudly about the amount of
data entry necessary to make an online purchase. Any shortcut that allowed
them to skip a step was welcome and using progressive disclosure to hide
optional form fields that are only infrequently used (such as Address Line 2)
makes the form appear shorter and more manageable to users.
Many sites recognized that entering one’s address was time-consuming,
error-prone work for users, especially on mobile, and instituted various auto-
complete mechanisms; some worked well, such as asking for a user’s ZIP
code and populating the city and state, while still allowing the user to correct
the occasional automated error. However, other designs such as the Google
Maps lookup that was used on many sites via API, auto-populated an address
in a way that prevented users from changing the automatically entered data.
This issue often occurred when users accidentally selected the wrong address
from a list, or if the autocompleted address didn’t match the user’s actual
address.

Optional Registration
Shoppers don’t want to register. They are tired of usernames and passwords.
They don’t want to “join” sites they don’t intend to visit frequently. They
worry registration will cause unwanted email or physical mail. They often see
registration as a benefit for the site (keeping their personal information)
rather than for them (making future purchases or package tracking easier.)
One user said, “I don’t like having to register on every single website for
every single purchase that I make. I’d rather do it as a guest and then I’m
not subject to their constant emails. I just prefer to get my item and go.”
Users appreciated optional registration that was part of the checkout flow,
towards the end. Registration can be as simple as adding optional password
fields to the existing purchase process, with a note like “Save time, next
time. Create a password and we’ll store your details”. Listing the benefits of
registration from the shoppers’ point of view, rather than the site’s point of
view, helped. Users liked to know they could save time or track orders.

Registration was optional on OfficeMax.com. Users could check out as a guest
with the option to register for the site in the checkout process.
Emphasizing the ability to save information for quicker future purchases is
important. In our first study, users often complained about providing

personal information because of security concerns. In our second study,
shoppers were tired of entering the same information repeatedly on each site
they visited. Remind users that registration means quicker checkout and less

Moosejaw offered returning users who had previously checked out as a guest
the option to enter a password and store their saved details.
Money Matters
Users didn’t want to be surprised with additional charges or fees at the end
of a checkout process. They appreciated knowing the full price early.
They also wanted easy ways to save money. They wanted specials that were
advertised on the site to be automatically applied to an order, rather than
having to remember or enter a coupon code. They wanted to see how
discounts or savings were applied before entering payment information. And
they wanted to see the full breakdown of their costs before entering that
payment information.
Shipping Options
Many users appreciated being able to go to a store to pick up an item
purchased online. Such options bypassed many of users’ complaints about

93

shopping online: shipping charges, waiting for delivery, and being home to
accept a package.
Regardless what the shipping options were, users wanted to know associated
costs, estimated delivery timeframe, and, in some cases, which carrier would
deliver the items. Some users, particularly in our New York City research,
had a strong preference for certain carriers over others.
Shipping costs needed to be easy to find. Some users comparison shopped
for the best deals on shipping, to get the most for their money. Others
looked for free shipping offers. Users complained about shipping charges as
one of the big downsides of online shopping, so free shipping offers were
enticing and appreciated.

## Vol.05: Search

Search Behavior
Across all of our studies, 85% of participants searched on at least one site
(140 out of 165 people).
Users had many different reasons to search on these sites. The ten main
reasons we observed were:
•
To get a feeling for the breadth or depth of the site’s product selection
•
To quickly locate a product category, instead of using site navigation
•
After unsuccessfully using the site navigation
•
To locate a specific product
•
To locate products with a specific attribute
•
When product descriptions did not answer shoppers’ questions
•
When faceted search or guided navigation did not return the desired
product results
•
To narrow product options
•
To return to an item previously located
•
When looking for a piece of information, rather than a product, on the
site
Of in-site searches, the most common type of query (33%) was a
combination of a product category and at least one product criterion, such as

“women’s ski pant.” Twenty-two percent of queries were for criteria alone
(such as “peanut”) and 25% were for categories (such as “shredders”). The
average in-site search query was 14.3 characters long, with 2.3 words.
Ecommerce search tools need to accommodate all sorts of queries, including
category names, product names, and product attributes as well as customer
service-related information.
Search Success
As we’ve conducted studies over the years, we’ve seen steady increases in
the average success rates for users when they search. In our most recent
study, 92% of searches resulted in relevant results that satisfied users.
As a result, it is no longer enough to just have a search feature that people
can use successfully. To complete, you need to provide a seamless, smooth
experience, which makes people feel in complete control of their options.
Search as Feedback
All sites can benefit from regularly reviewing search logs. The search box is
the easiest way for users to talk to the site. Searches can reveal problem
areas in the navigation, gaps in selection and, most important, the language
that visitors use to describe your products and their needs.
Presenting Search Results
Search results need to clearly display products (or site content) that relates
to the user’s query. Some searches in our studies failed to deliver successful
results because they:
•
Contained insufficient information for the user to select the right
product
•
Presented a list that appeared to be in random order to the user
•
Included items that appeared to be — or actually were —
unrelated to the user’s search criteria
•
Contained too many items
•
Contained only one item
•
Misled users into thinking fewer items were available because of
poor presentation of promoted results
•
Had incomprehensible or inadequate page-to-page navigation
within the results

•
Presented irrelevant, poorly placed, or hard to use faceted search
options
Successful search results are a matter of the quality of the results returned
as well as how those results are presented.
Narrowing the Choices: Faceted Search
Sites can help users find the right products by helping them narrow their
choices. Many ecommerce sites offer faceted search (also called filters).
Users are presented with products and then given a list of product
characteristics by which to narrow their choices. Rather than displaying 200
digital cameras and only allowing users to sort by price and popularity,
faceted search allows users to pick the criteria most relevant to them.

When done well, faceted search tools like these on B&H Photo helped guide
users to the most relevant product choices.
Such options can be great — when they work. But there are many filters that
are poorly implemented. A key problem is categories that don’t match
shoppers’ interests or needs for that particular product. Sites also suffered
when they incorrectly categorized items, so users’ searches didn’t return all
products that met their requirements or returned items that didn’t match
users’ needs. Users needed to be able to easily notice and use options,
combining criteria when relevant and easily removing criteria.

96
Key Findings By Report

Scoped Search and Advanced Search
These two complex approaches to search should be avoided for most
ecommerce sites. It is generally better to let users narrow results rather than
ask them to scope the search or define many criteria when they submit the
query. For most sites, faceted search is a better alternative to scoped or
advanced search.
There are a few unique exceptions to this rule. For sites with enormous,
diverse product offerings (like Walmart or Amazon), a scoped search may be
necessary. Additionally, some travel sites (like Delta or Expedia) may need to
expose some advanced search criteria (like number of flight connections) up
front as users enter their query.

## Vol.06: Customer Service
**Tags:** customer-service, FAQ, contact, live-chat, help, returns

### Hallazgos clave

Further information about each of the following topics and additional topics
can be found in the Customer Service report in the Ecommerce Report
Series.
Findability
The most important aspect of providing customer service information is
making the information easy to find. If you have great customer service
content, but users are unable to find it when they need it, then you don’t
have good customer service, and this is how users will see it.
Users looked to the top and bottom of the page when looking for customer
service information. They looked for familiar links to high-level Customer
Service sections. Properly organizing this information and naming links
correctly is crucial to getting users to the information they need. Common
information requests, such as shipping or returns, can be separate links to
lead users directly to the information of interest.
Users also expected support information to be included throughout the site,
in relevant areas. They expected to see information about shipping or returns
as they were shopping and adding items to the shopping cart. Observe the
customer’s shopping experience, understand what questions users have at
various points in their activity. Produce and provide the right supplemental
content and expose it at key points in the workflow to provide contextual
help to customers when they need it.

97

Promoting Policies and Tools
Users noticed and responded positively to promoted, customer-friendly
policies on sites. Banners promoting free shipping, free returns, and
satisfaction or price guarantees were worth advertising throughout the site.
If your organization has policies that differentiate you from competitors,
make these helpful benefits work in your favor. Promote them in the right
places but be extra careful that you are setting appropriate expectations. If
users read the fine print or discover uncommunicated exceptions to policies,
these efforts can backfire.
A Good Site Experience in General
The best customer service is providing the information users want, when
they want it. This extends throughout the site beyond the customer
experience content pages. This includes providing full and complete product
information, clear product images, detailed shopping carts, and a logical
checkout process. An all-around good user experience from beginning to end
is the biggest customer service benefit you can offer. This, in turn creates
loyalty and repeat sales.
Getting in Touch
When shoppers needed an answer to their questions, some wanted to help
themselves through the website and others wanted a way to contact the
company directly.
Even users who did not need to contact the company appreciated seeing a
freely provided phone number. This indicated to users that the company was
easy to contact if they encountered a problem. Sites that make it difficult to
find a phone number make customers question the company’s agenda and
negatively influenced users’ perceptions of the company.
It’s important to provide a variety of contact options. Don’t force all users
through a contact form. Different questions and issues call for different
means of contact. For most users, email means lag-time, and in urgent
situations, this will not suffice. In those situations where waiting for a
response is acceptable, email was a nice option, so they wouldn’t have to
wait on hold. Bottom-line, providing options is key. When customers have a
question, making it difficult to get an answer can exacerbate any problem
they may have, and this can have negative long-term impacts on customer
perception.
Live help, via chat, was used very heavily in our most recent study. Users
have become accustomed to immediacy on the web, and live chat delivers on
that expectation. Many users would seek out live chat immediately when

98
Key Findings By Report

questions arose, preferring to ask a quick question while doing other things
over attempting to navigate to find the answer on their own. Users had high
expectations for these interactions, expecting the representatives to provide
more details or insight than they could find on the site on their own.
However, live chat can pose issues if it interrupts and annoys users when
they don’t need it. Be sure to position it and promote it in the right way.
Clear and Customer-Friendly Policies
Once users found policies, their job was not finished. They had to read and
understand the policies, and any exceptions to them. This was difficult on
several sites, where policies and exclusions were unclear or grossly
overcomplicated
The policies themselves had to be customer-friendly, as well. Users were
bothered by technical jargon, unfamiliar branded terminology, self-serving
policies, and complex exceptions.
Users also compared your policies to competitors. Many ecommerce sites
have raised the stakes when it comes providing perks to customers. In
recent years, free shipping, fast shipping, open return policies are becoming
the norm. Evaluate if your policies can compete with your competitors.
Consider the customer when creating policies and their associated
procedures. Help users whenever possible such as, providing return mailing
labels or allowing local returns.

## Vol.07: Selling Strategies
**Tags:** pricing, promotions, cross-sell, upsell, shipping-cost, urgency, scarcity

### Hallazgos clave

Further information about each of the following topics and additional topics
can be found in the Selling Strategies report in the Ecommerce Report
Series.
Clear Pricing
Online shoppers are often price-conscious. The value of items must be clearly
communicated, so users know what they are getting for their money. Product
prices need to be clear, and savings and discounts need to be reflected. All
additional charges should be listed and explained as early as possible in the
process.
Shoppers also wanted easy ways to see sale items. A Sale section was
helpful, but shoppers frequently also wanted to see sale items interwoven
with the main site inventory, seeing full-price and sale items side by side.

99

When Will It Arrive
Shoppers wanted to know how quickly an item would arrive. This was often a
combination of availability, packaging and shipping. Users appreciated sites
with clear timeframes, even if it was a window of a few days. This set user
expectations for delivery.
Free Shipping
As mentioned previously, free shipping was a good incentive for many
shoppers. Shoppers often complained about paying for shipping, especially
when many users shopped online in order to get a better price on a product.
They often compared the online price to the cost and effort involved in
buying the same item from a local store. Whether offers were constant on
the site, or available for a limited time or with a minimum purchase, many
shoppers found such offers enticing. We watched many users change their
orders or find additional items on a site, so they could reach the minimum
total to receive free shipping. They wanted to spend their money on
products, not on shipping.
Welcome Back
Sites need to accommodate both new and returning customers. Sites can
support returning shoppers not just through personal information saved via
registration. The site can highlight new or discounted products, letting
shoppers know what’s changed since a previous visit. Information from
previous visits can be used to promote products, and shopping carts can be
remembered.
One user said on United.com: “I like United because I have used it before
and when I log in, they remember the airports I use most often.”
You Might Also Like ...
When we first studied ecommerce sites, users weren’t thrilled with the idea
of the upsell. They didn’t appreciate when sites offered them something
related or directed them to other products they might like. In our latest
research, users counted on, expected, and used these recommendations, in
some cases using related products as a primary method of navigating
through a site’s inventory.
Users appreciated product listings that explained why a product was
recommended. Labels explaining that items were related, recommended
accessories, or products other customers purchased helped shoppers know
why an item was being shown. One user said as he viewed a list of popular
products, “I’m sure other people buy good things. I’ll look at these.”

100
Key Findings By Report

They were open to such offers on product pages but did not appreciate offers
that interfered with the shopping or checkout process.
One Shopping Experience: Online & Offline, Desktop & Mobile
Users expect to see the same products and features when using a website on
a computer, phone, or tablet. When stores had online and physical
presences, users commonly moved between the two. Users didn’t think of
shopping online as distinct from shopping offline – they simply thought of
shopping. They related many instances where they combined online and
offline experiences when making a purchase. However, in many cases,
websites and stores may have different inventory or policies. When this is the
case, it needs to be clear to users through appropriate inventory
specifications indicating where an item is and is not available.
The line between ecommerce and physical shopping has become increasingly
blurred, and the onus lies with businesses to communicate any disparities
between the two.
Social Shopping
Shopping is often a social activity, and shoppers depend on the opinions of
others when they are uncertain about a product or service. Alternatively,
they may arrive on a site via the testimonial of a friend, or a friend of a
friend on a social media site, or because they were directed to an ecommerce
site through a targeted advertising campaign on a social media site. It’s
critical to account for social sharing and to offer sharing mechanisms that are
appropriate for the audience and product inventory.

Gift Giving Online
Purchasing gifts is a complex and often stressful task for many people.
Deciding how much to spend, not knowing other people’s tastes, and figuring
out how to deliver a gift to someone else are all complicated issues. There is
uncertainty about spending money on items that you wouldn’t choose for

101

yourself, and therefore aren’t certain how to evaluate whether or not it’s the
right fit.
When it comes to gift-giving, you need to cater to a broader audience than
your primary customers. For example, even if you’re selling fashionable
clothes for young people, a gift-giving shopper might be a grandparent
buying for her grandson. Thus, to realize the site’s full benefits, your product
pages and checkout process must be understandable and clear to first-time
buyers outside your target audience.
Buying gifts online requires that users are able to first find popular items that
make for good gifts (even if they’re not familiar with your product line).
Then, your site has to make the gift customization process easy enough that
users feel confident that they know what the recipient’s unboxing experience
will be like. Will the recipient get a nicely printed card with the name of the
gift-giver placed on top of a well-wrapped package, or will they get their item
in a chintzy gift bag with no note indicating who sent the mysterious
package? Make this process clear and explicit for anxious gift-givers during
the checkout process.
Wishlists as Favorites, Not Necessarily Wish Lists
Many people in our study found the idea of a wishlist for sharing gift ideas
with others as tacky or being potentially perceived as greedy. However, the
concept of saving product ideas for later purchase was a nearly universal
behavior among participants in our study, typically using the shopping cart
as a temporary holding area. This demonstrates that users often need a
place to store items for later consideration. For many sites, it’s better to
present wishlists as favorites lists (that can, of course, be later shared with
other people for gift giving holidays).
Well-designed wishlist features typically used names like “favorites,” “saved
items,” or, “my list,” focusing on ease of adding items to a list for later
consideration. However, once a user has added items to a list, offer easy
ways to share this with others.
Gift Cards
Gift cards were seen as a last-minute option by many participants, or one
they chose when hopelessly lost for what to buy. They felt guilty when giving
them, but interestingly, most participants admitted to enjoying receiving
them. For recipients with different tastes, gift cards allowed participants in
the study to dictate which store the gift would be bought from, while leaving
the choice of item up to the recipient. This was definitely perceived as better
than sending cash in the mail by both senders and recipients.

Users put more trust into sites that looked clean or professional, rather than

## Vol.10: International Purchasers
**Tags:** international, localization, currency, language, shipping-international, forms

### Hallazgos clave

Further information about each of the following topics and additional topics
can be found in the International Purchasers report in the Ecommerce Report
Series.
Language & Currency
It was helpful when sites detected users’ country location and/or browser
language setting and automatically defaulted to the corresponding country-
specific site. This saved users from the extra step of having to manually
chose their country before entering a site. But sites also needed to be sure
that shoppers could easily change the country, language, and currency, if
needed.
For example, some shoppers in Mexico and Chile preferred to pay with US
Dollars if the exchange rate was better that way, so they wanted to be able
to separate the currency from the Spanish language setting.
In our most recent round of research, some sites included an option to
translate reviews written in another language. This was particularly helpful
on travel sites.
Localized Forms & Payment Methods
In this latest research, we observed some improvements to checkout forms
for international users. Fewer sites made the mistake of asking for US states
and ZIP Codes for international customers. And more sites helped users input
their phone numbers by using country-selectors and flexible phone number
fields that accommodated long international number formats. Such
improvements may seem small, but they dramatically improve the user
experience for international shoppers.
Beyond forms, more sites accommodated alternative payment methods that
were appropriate for international customers. For example, some countries
used PayPal more than credit cards, so both options were available to users.
Credit cards aren’t widely used in many countries around the world, so sites
that understood this and offered alternative payment methods were open to
a wider range of customers.
Customer Support for International Shoppers
Traditional methods for contacting customer support, such as email and
phone, should be offered, but in our latest research, we observed some users
preferring to contact companies via social media or 24/7 live chat. These

104
Key Findings By Report

were often more convenient for international customers because they already
had the apps installed (for instance, Instagram, Facebook, or WhatsApp),
and they didn’t have to worry about long-distance or toll-free dialing. Sites
that made it easy to find the company on social media had an advantage of
being more accessible to international purchasers.
One area that hasn’t seen much improvement between rounds of research is
shipping and delivery for international purchases. Some sites would ship
items internationally, but they provided little support. Many international
users complained about not being able to know in advance if an item would
get stuck in customs or arrive with a surprise customs fee upon delivery.
Sites generally only gave vague warnings that customs and duties may
apply, but this wasn’t reassuring to users, especially because often the extra
fees cost more than the item itself.
The lack of free methods of contact deterred some shoppers (another reason
why social media support mentioned above is so helpful), and others worried
about the hassle of dealing with international returns or exchanges,
particularly on sites with strict policies. Other users complained when they
did not qualify for discounts or special offers based on their location.

We recommend testing in international markets that are key to the business.
Run testing locally in target countries, preferably in the local language.

## Vol.11: Store Finders & Locators
**Tags:** store-locator, maps, locations, omnichannel, pickup

### Hallazgos clave

Further information about each of the following topics and additional topics
can be found in the Store Finders and Locators report in the Ecommerce
Report Series.
Locator Tools
Customers often need to visit companies in the physical world, and this
affects business success in a very simple way: they can either find your
locations or they cannot. The locator tool on a website or app guides users to
physical locations. People use locators to:
•
Find the location closest to their home, work, or current location
•
Collect information on a specific location, such as its address,
phone number, hours of operation, and products/services
•
Get directions to a specific location

105

Having a well-designed locator feature is extremely important for companies
that have many — or any — physical locations and need to effectively
disseminate this information to employees, clients, and customers.
Successfully using a locator involves three steps:
•
Finding the locator
•
Using the locator to find your desired location
•
Getting directions from your starting point to this location
All three of these steps must be easy. Step 2 is the main culprit in usability
problems these days: The actual locators are either too complex, or they are
oversimplified, making it more difficult for users to find which locations meet
their needs.
People Gravitate to Search and Mapping Apps
The vast majority of users in our studies went directly to a search engine
when we asked them to find a nearby location for a specific company.
Similarly, mobile users rely on mapping apps (native or non-native) on their
phones to search for nearby locations. This search-dominant behavior raises
a question: Should you eliminate the location finder from your own site, since
so many people turn to search first? The answer is no, because as soon as
users already on your site decide to visit your physical location they should
be able to easily access a location finder, and even those who start out on a
search engine often navigate to the site’s locator tool to gather specific
details about various locations.
Locator Links
One of the most critical factors for users’ success when finding locations is
having an obvious link to the locator on the site’s homepage or throughout
the site in the global navigation. A clear label for the link, such as “Find a
Store” or “Locations,” is key.
Interactive Locator Tool vs. Location List
The number of locations you have should dictate the kind of locator feature
you create for your website or app. If you have only a few locations, it isn’t
worth the cost of creating full location search and parameterization; in fact,
doing so might confuse rather than help users. With few (2-10) locations,
having a location directory listing all location information would work well,
rather than a location search tool.

106
Key Findings By Report

Location Search
For businesses with many locations, searching for an appropriate location
must be simple and straightforward. Users expect to be able to enter a
location — or have the site or app detect their current location — and have
the tool find places for them, rather than looking through a directory-style
list. When locations differ in regard to services, amenities, or products
available, filters should be provided to allow users to find locations based on
these parameters.
Dominance of External-Mapping Tools for Getting Directions
The reliance on external mapping tools, such as Google Maps or mobile
native mapping apps, to provide turn-by-turn directions to locations has
increased over recent years (as opposed to custom mapping and directions
tools). This movement to leverage external tools is helpful to users because
they are able to finish their task of determining how to get to a physical
location using a familiar tool instead of learning a new interface on every
website they visit.

## Vol.12: Transactional Email
**Tags:** email, confirmation, notifications, order-status, shipping-email

### Hallazgos clave

#### Messages

Goals of Transactional Email
Transactional emails must serve their primary purpose — delivering a
message to a customer about their account, registration, purchase, booking,
or other action. To that end, transactional email should have three goals:
1.
Avoid being mistaken for spam or unnecessary marketing
messages. Email must survive users’ ruthless pruning of inbox
messages.
2.
Be a customer service ambassador. Email should enhance a
company’s reputation for customer service and increase users’
confidence in their dealings with the company.

107

3.
Prevent customers from calling or emailing customer service, which
can be expensive. However, do not simply eliminate phone contact
information in favor of email. Some users will still need to reach out
by phone and taking away this option undermines the previous
goal.
All three goals are important, but if an email message fails the first goal, it
also automatically fails the other two simply because people won’t read it. To
ensure your email isn’t immediately deleted, carefully craft the From and
Subject fields.
In most cases, the From field (also called the sender information) should
show a recognizable brand name (if available).
We saw many Subject fields (also called subject lines) that worked well, and
many that did not. The main differentiator was the degree to which the
subject line was explicitly related to a customer-initiated transaction.
Participants deleted email with subject lines that seemed too vague or much
like spam (such as “Important information”).
The very best subject lines were so good that users didn’t open the message.
For example, “Your Amazon.com order of “WEICHUAN 10 Pieces Large ...’
has shipped!” This subject line was so detailed, users were fully informed
without having to open the message.
This is fine if your message contains no additional information that requires
the user’s immediate attention. Many of our participants said they’d save this
type of message and open it only if the package didn’t arrive and they
needed the tracking number.
A good subject line is gold. Invest accordingly in writing the copy.
Transactional-Email Content
When people decide to open an email message, they want to know the
message’s point immediately, especially if it’s not explicitly stated in the
subject line. People appreciate email that saves them time and gets straight
to the point.
People read longer email messages if the content seemed useful or important
to them. Marketing or informal language caused some people to stop
reading. People were also irritated by useless information or other “fluff”
displayed before the essential content. It made them want to stop reading,
but they were concerned that they might miss important information that
followed.
In general, you should write a message according to the users’ priorities,
starting with the information they’re typically looking for. Email that begins
with marketing messages or other seemingly irrelevant information runs a

108
Key Findings By Report

major risk of being deleted, because people might never scroll down to see
the information they need.
Email and Trust
Good email that respects users’ time and quickly tells them what they need
to know can do wonders for your customer service reputation. People don’t
always trust websites, but when they get a confirmation message, it seems
like something is actually happening.
Common Problems with Transactional Email
Our findings identified several frequent problems with transactional email:

Incomplete information
•
Impersonal or form letters

Lack of company contact information

Unanswered questions

•
Wasting customers’ time

Sending too much email

## Protocolo de uso

1. **Búsqueda local primero:** El agente busca en este índice por tags o palabras clave relevantes al proyecto.
2. **Citación:** Cada hallazgo usado se cita como `[NNGROUP: Vol.XX · sección · hallazgo específico]`.
3. **Actualización web:** Después de consultar la base local, el agente busca en `site:nngroup.com` con WebSearch para encontrar artículos más recientes que complementen o actualicen los hallazgos.
4. **Match local↔web:** Si un hallazgo de la base local tiene una versión actualizada online, el agente cita ambos: la base local como fundamento y el artículo web como actualización.
5. **Nuevos hallazgos:** Si la búsqueda web encuentra guidelines que NO están en la base local, se citan como `[NNGROUP-WEB: título artículo · fecha · URL]`.
